\section{Introduction}
The Romanian housing market has experienced dramatic trends in the last 5 years, with residential prices in major urban hubs like Bucharest and Cluj-Napoca surging by over 40\%. In this vibrant and essential market, it is important that we establish AI models that are able to reliably predict the price of real estate, in order to decide if the price of certain offers is too high or too low.
Unfortunately, we weren't able to find a state of the art model for the Bucharest housing market, since financial data and algorithms tend to be proprietary, however consistent with typical data science benchmarks, our evaluation demonstrates that \textbf{XGBoost} yields the highest performance on this dataset. Our solution also found other interesting patterns in the data, such as a 73\% correlation between the price of a unit and the space it has to offer.

\section{Dataset description}
Our dataset documents the real estate market in Bucharest, specifically for apartments.
Our dataset contains \textbf{16{,}022} rows and \textbf{12} columns. Each column is:
\begin{itemize}
    \bfseries
    \item Price
    \item Surface 
    \item Nr. of rooms
    \item Floor 
    \item Max Floor (how many floors the building has)
    \item Year built
    \item Neighbourhood (what district it is in Bucharest)
    \item Elevator (Yes/No)
    \item Construction Material 
    \item Nr. of bathrooms
    \item Latitude 
    \item Longitude 
    \item Metro Proximity (Distance to the nearest subway station)
    \item STB Proximity (Distance to public transit in general)
\end{itemize}

All of the columns have positive values, except for -1, which designated \texttt{N/A}.

\section{Dataset production}
We have decided to limit our crawling to flats, not houses, that are in Bucharest. This approach allows us to have more homogenous data, facilitating the tasks of our algorithms.
 We set a target of 10000 different samples from 2 websites : \emph{imobiliare.ro} and \emph{storia.ro}. This equal split is able to correct biases related to the platform, but also make sure that we have enough data for the Machine Learning section. In order to obtain all the relevant data from each website, we first notice that all the listings are make available using pagination (e.g. on page 1 there are 10 listing , on page 2 there are 10 listing and so forth). After we gather 10 000 links/listings, each property is analysed using an automated script. On \emph{storia.ro}, there are no rate limits for crawling, so everything could be done in one session, however \emph{imobiliare.ro} imposes limits on crawling, resulting in a process conducted in multiple sessions.

 For each listing, the following relevant attributes were collected:

\begin{itemize}
    \bfseries
    \item URL \texttt{String}
    \item Price \texttt{Int}
    \item Surface \texttt{Float}
    \item Nr. of rooms \texttt{Int}
    \item Floor \texttt{Int}
    \item Max Floor \texttt{Int}
    \item Year built \texttt{Int}
    \item City \texttt{String}
    \item Elevator \texttt{Bool}
    \item Construction Material \texttt{String}
    \item Nr. of bathrooms \texttt{Int}
    \item Latitude \texttt{Float}
    \item Longitude \texttt{Float}
    \item Metro Proximity \texttt{Float}
    \item STB Proximity \texttt{Float}
\end{itemize}
 
 Metro Proximity represents how close the listing is to the nearest metro station, and STB Proximity how close it was to any other public transit. They were computed using the latitude and the longitude of the listing, and comparing it to all the latitudes and longitudes of all the  stations.

Furthermore, we also decided to collect how many floors the building has, as there is an inverse relationship between how many floors there are in total and the price.

\subsection{Data Clean-up}

As there are no limits on the price, surface, and number of rooms, we want to limit the data to more normal listings that are also serious in nature. A series of constraints was established to filter out the data:
\begin{itemize}
    \item $1100 \text{ \euro} /\text{ m}^2< \frac{\texttt{price}}{\texttt{surface}} < 6000 \text{ \euro} /\text{ m}^2$
    \item $ 25 \text{ m}^2<\texttt{surface} < 200 \text{ m}^2$
    \item $ \texttt{stb\_proximity} < 2 \text{km}$
\end{itemize}

By enforcing these constraints, we ensure that each listing in our dataset isn't too outlandish and normalises our data. For example, if the price per square meter would be above 7000 \euro, an apartment of $130 \text{m}^2$ would cost 910000 \euro which is nearly a million. In Bucharest, this is typically found only in luxury developments such as BHB Avenue, One Lake Club, and One Mircea Eliade. These projects are located in some of the city's most prestigious areas and offer extensive amenities, including gyms, spas, saunas, indoor swimming pools, and ground-floor restaurants.

Another problem we had was treating \texttt{N/A}s. We replaced them with -1, as all of our data is positive. Furthermore, we found that on \emph{storia.ro} the number of bathrooms was always 1. This could be due to the fact that there isn't a field specifically designed for the number of bathrooms on the website, and many sellers choose not to put the number of bathrooms in the description.


After performing this clean-up, we were left with \textbf{1622} listings and \textbf{12} columns.

\section{Dimensionality Reduction}

To prepare the dataset for various machine learning models and eliminate potentially redundant information, we employed three distinct dimensionality reduction and feature selection techniques: Principal Component Analysis (\textbf{PCA}), Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (\textbf{UMAP}), and \textbf{Lasso} Regression. Prior to applying these techniques, a robust preprocessing pipeline was implemented: numerical features were imputed using the median and scaled using \texttt{StandardScaler}, while categorical features were imputed with the most frequent value and encoded using \texttt{OneHotEncoder}.

\subsection{Principal Component Analysis (PCA)}
For PCA, we wanted to retain the most significant variance of the dataset while reducing the feature space. We configured the PCA algorithm to capture 95\% of the variance. This linear transformation projected the high-dimensional preprocessed data into a smaller set of uncorrelated variables (10 principal components).

\subsection{Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP)}
To capture potential non-linear relationships in our data, we utilised UMAP. Unlike PCA, which focuses on global variance, UMAP attempts to preserve both the local and global topological structure of the data. We constrained the algorithm to reduce the feature space to exactly 6 dimensions. 

\subsection{Lasso Feature Selection}
Rather than projecting the data into a new mathematical space like PCA and UMAP, Lasso (Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator) was used for explicit feature selection via $L_1$ regularization. We used a 5-fold cross-validation approach (\texttt{LassoCV}) evaluating 50 penalty values ($\alpha$) distributed logarithmically between $10^{-2}$ and $10^4$. The optimal $\alpha$ was identified by minimizing MSE. Features whose coefficients shrank to exactly zero were discarded, while the remaining non-zero features (12 original dimensions) were retained.

\section{Results}
As shown in table~\ref{tab:2}, \textbf{XGBoost} was by far the best algorithm for this task. It trained in a relatively short period of time, and its error was by far the best. Anther benefit that this technique has is that it does not require regularisation, and can natively handle \texttt{N/A}s.
Another interesting pattern is that decision trees based algorithms generally outperformed regression/linear models. The Multilayer perceptron (\textbf{MLP}) performed overall very poorly. Not only did it require more time then all the other techniques, it is only marginally better then a standard random forest. This confirm the principle, that Neural Networks really shine when a lot of data is available, but on smaller datasets traditional data science techniques work better.
As \textbf{XGBoost} has a RMSE of only 35{,}000 and a MAE of only 19{,}300 , it is a surprisingly succesful approach and we find it fit to be production-ready. The only limitations about this approach are that the photos of the apartment are not available to the algorithm. Further research would require giving \textbf{XGBoost} a vector that encodes the images of each listing, further improving accuracy. 


Another interesting pattern is that \textbf{Lasso} is better than \textbf{PCA} wich is better than \textbf{UMAP}. We expect that this discrepency is due to the number of dimentions that we set to be reduced: \textbf{Lasso} has 12 dimensions, \textbf{PCA} has 10 dimensions and \textbf{UMAP} has 6 dimensions. This highlights that the final versions of our dataset does not have redundant columns and even if it had \textbf{XGBoost} would handle them with no loss of performance.    

\subsection{Further analysis}
We analysed the worst predictions of \textbf{XGBoost}, both in overestimating the price of a listing but also in underestimating. We expect that they are related to the state of the apartment, such as how much furniture it has. Some of the listings it had a bad performance genuinely seemed very overpriced. However the majority of them were due to the lack of image encoding. For instance, in Figure~\ref{fig:8}, \textbf{XGBoost} overestimated the value of the unit by 220{,}000 \euro \space, because it wasn't able to see the dubious plumbing job that the house received and also the rotting walls. The housing unit also appears to have broken windows that were lazily replaced with cardboard. In contrast, Figure~\ref{fig:9} shows a housing unit that was underestimated by 380{,}000 \euro. The housing unit is full of luxurious furniture, and the apartment also comes with private security. We didn't have the budget to obtain image encodings of each listings, and besides the big cost it would also take a lot of time. Another approach is to see how much the performance scales when we augment the dataset to contain a lot of data. We had \textbf{16{,}022} listings, and because of that \textbf{XGBoost} performs best, but if we had more then 100{,}000 maybe the \textbf{MLP} would achieve the best performance.

\section{Conclusion}
The Bucharest housing market has a lot of noticeable patterns that impact pricing, such as a 73\% correlation between the space a housing unit has to offer and its price. Recently built housing units are especially expensive, and the only luxurious residential area appears to be Aviatorilor, with more affordable housing on the edge of the city. As shown above, real estate developers love to build near existing transit infrastructure, such as the subway system. \textbf{XGBoost} offers the best performance on the dataset, with a RMSE of approximately 35{,}000 \euro \space which is very good considering it doesn't have acces to a lot of relevant information, such as the pictures.
\newline
\subsection{Future approaches}
A very interesting direction would be to encode the images of the listings using an image encoder, which would allow our algorithm to notice the general state of the unit (i.e. furniture, walls etc.).
Another interesting remark is that our algorithms only analyse the price of \textbf{listed} apartments, not \textbf{sold} apartments. For example, the price could change multiple times in the future before it gets sold. We addressed these concerns by removing obviously absurd offers, but having a dataset with the actual price the house was sold would make a more accurate case study.
Another limitation of our approach was that we could have included more fields in our dataset, such as if the house has air conditioning, or if parking is available underneath the structure. Including such information can only increase the accuracy of this approach, but would be tedious and expensive since sellers are not required to make such information available on their post.
